# EST5EDT vs America/New_York in PostgreSQL

<Authors date="November 16, 2026">

`EST5EDT`, `CST6CDT`, `MST7MDT` and `PST8PDT` look like convenient names for the four main US time zones, and plenty of old configuration files, Java properties and database settings still use them. For years they were simply aliases: in the time zone database (tzdata) they pointed to `America/New_York`, `America/Chicago`, `America/Denver` and `America/Los_Angeles`, so it did not matter which name you used.

That stopped being true with tzdata 2026f. According to the PostgreSQL 18.7 release notes, these four zones "now use their indicated fixed UTC offsets and abbreviations for all historical timestamps" instead of inheriting the history of the city-named zones. PostgreSQL 18.7, 17.12, 16.16, 15.20 and 14.25 (released November 12, 2026) bundle tzdata 2026f, so after a minor update, PostgreSQL can return a different local time for the same old timestamp depending on which of the two names you use. The same tzdata release also moved [Manitoba and the Northwest Territories to year-round time](/blog/postgres-manitoba-time-change-2026). This article measures the difference, explains who is affected, and shows how to move settings and data to the city-named zones.

## What These Names Mean

A name like `EST5EDT` is shaped like a POSIX `TZ` string: a standard-time abbreviation (`EST`), the offset in hours **west** of UTC (`5`), and a daylight-time abbreviation (`EDT`). Unlike a full POSIX string, it does not say when daylight time starts and ends. tzdata defines these four names as zones of their own, and since 2026f they follow US federal daylight saving rules throughout history, with no local exceptions.

City-named zones such as `America/New_York` follow what clocks in that city actually did. That includes local mean time before standard time zones were adopted in 1883, and the decades between the World Wars when New York observed daylight time under local law while there was no national rule.

There are also fixed-offset names, such as `EST` (always UTC-5, no daylight time), and full POSIX specifications such as `EST5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0` that spell out the transition rules. Those behave differently again, as shown below.

## Measuring the Difference

We compared both names for every hour from 1900 to 2030, on PostgreSQL 18.6 (which bundles tzdata 2026c) and on 18.7 (2026f):

```sql
SET timezone = 'UTC';
SELECT count(*) FILTER (WHERE (g AT TIME ZONE 'EST5EDT') <> (g AT TIME ZONE 'America/New_York'))    AS est_vs_ny,
       count(*) FILTER (WHERE (g AT TIME ZONE 'PST8PDT') <> (g AT TIME ZONE 'America/Los_Angeles')) AS pst_vs_la,
       count(*) FILTER (WHERE (g AT TIME ZONE 'CST6CDT') <> (g AT TIME ZONE 'America/Chicago'))     AS cst_vs_chi,
       count(*) FILTER (WHERE (g AT TIME ZONE 'MST7MDT') <> (g AT TIME ZONE 'America/Denver'))      AS mst_vs_den,
       min(g) FILTER (WHERE (g AT TIME ZONE 'EST5EDT') <> (g AT TIME ZONE 'America/New_York'))      AS first_diff,
       max(g) FILTER (WHERE (g AT TIME ZONE 'EST5EDT') <> (g AT TIME ZONE 'America/New_York'))      AS last_diff
FROM generate_series(timestamptz '1900-01-01', timestamptz '2030-12-31', interval '1 hour') g;
```

PostgreSQL 18.6:

```
 est_vs_ny | pst_vs_la | cst_vs_chi | mst_vs_den | first_diff | last_diff
-----------+-----------+------------+------------+------------+-----------
         0 |         0 |          0 |          0 |            |
```

PostgreSQL 18.7:

```
 est_vs_ny | pst_vs_la | cst_vs_chi | mst_vs_den |       first_diff       |       last_diff
-----------+-----------+------------+------------+------------------------+------------------------
    168461 |     73222 |     170645 |      15620 | 1920-03-28 07:00:00+00 | 1966-10-30 05:00:00+00
```

On 18.6 the names were identical over 131 years. On 18.7, `EST5EDT` and `America/New_York` disagree on 168,461 hours, all between 1920 and 1966. Chicago shows a similar count, Los Angeles fewer, and Denver the fewest, which matches how much local daylight saving each city observed in that period.

### Sample Timestamps

Converting a handful of instants into both names on 18.7 shows the pattern:

```sql
SET timezone = 'UTC';
SELECT ts, ts AT TIME ZONE 'EST5EDT' AS est5edt, ts AT TIME ZONE 'America/New_York' AS new_york
FROM (VALUES (timestamptz '1883-11-18 12:00+00'), (timestamptz '1943-01-15 12:00+00'),
             (timestamptz '1950-07-01 12:00+00'), (timestamptz '1974-01-15 12:00+00'),
             (timestamptz '2027-01-15 12:00+00')) v(ts);
```

```
           ts           |       est5edt       |      new_york
------------------------+---------------------+---------------------
 1883-11-18 12:00:00+00 | 1883-11-18 07:00:00 | 1883-11-18 07:03:58
 1943-01-15 12:00:00+00 | 1943-01-15 08:00:00 | 1943-01-15 08:00:00
 1950-07-01 12:00:00+00 | 1950-07-01 07:00:00 | 1950-07-01 08:00:00
 1974-01-15 12:00:00+00 | 1974-01-15 08:00:00 | 1974-01-15 08:00:00
 2027-01-15 12:00:00+00 | 2027-01-15 07:00:00 | 2027-01-15 07:00:00
```

- **1883**: New York still used local mean time, 4 hours 56 minutes 2 seconds behind UTC. `EST5EDT` now shows a clean UTC-5.
- **1943**: both show 08:00. That was nationwide "War Time", a federal rule, so the federal-rules zone has it too.
- **1950**: New York observed summer daylight time under state and local law; there was no federal rule. `EST5EDT` now says standard time.
- **1974**: both show daylight time in January. That was the federal emergency daylight time of the energy crisis.
- **2027**: identical. Current and future rules match.

On 18.6, every row showed the same value in both columns, including `07:03:58` for 1883 and `08:00:00` for 1950.

The difference goes in both directions. Converting the wall-clock time `1950-07-01 08:00` to an instant gives `12:00 UTC` with `America/New_York` but `13:00 UTC` with `EST5EDT` on 18.7. And with `SET timezone = 'EST5EDT'`, `timestamptz '1950-07-01 12:00+00'` now displays as `1950-07-01 07:00:00-05` instead of `08:00:00-04`.

## Who Is Affected

Present-day and future timestamps are not affected; the US rules since 1967 are federal, so both names agree. You are affected if all of these are true:

1. A session, role, database or server uses `EST5EDT`, `CST6CDT`, `MST7MDT` or `PST8PDT` as its `TimeZone`, or an application passes one of them to `AT TIME ZONE` or a driver.
2. You store or display timestamps from before 1967 (roughly; the exact range depends on the city): historical archives, genealogy data, birth dates stored as `timestamptz`, insurance or pension records, scientific observations, or backfilled time series.
3. Something converts between local time and UTC using that name: rendering, parsing input, `date_trunc` by local day, grouping by local hour.

A birth date stored as `timestamptz '1950-07-01 00:00'` while the session zone was `EST5EDT` (and the rules were still the old ones) was saved as `04:00 UTC`. On 18.7 the same session renders it as `1950-06-30 23:00:00-05`, a different calendar day. This is the classic reason not to store dates as timestamps: a `date` column is immune to any time zone rule change.

## Finding Where the Names Are Used

Start with the settings. Check the current value and where it came from:

```sql
SELECT name, setting, source, sourcefile
FROM pg_settings
WHERE name IN ('TimeZone', 'log_timezone');
```

Per-database and per-role settings live in `pg_db_role_setting`:

```sql
SELECT coalesce(d.datname, '(all databases)') AS database,
       coalesce(r.rolname, '(all roles)')     AS role,
       s.setconfig
FROM pg_db_role_setting s
LEFT JOIN pg_database d ON d.oid = s.setdatabase
LEFT JOIN pg_roles    r ON r.oid = s.setrole
WHERE array_to_string(s.setconfig, ',') ~* 'timezone=(EST5EDT|CST6CDT|MST7MDT|PST8PDT)';
```

Then look for stored zone names. If your schema has columns such as `user_timezone` or `tz`, count the legacy values:

```sql
SELECT tz, count(*)
FROM appointments
WHERE tz IN ('EST5EDT', 'CST6CDT', 'MST7MDT', 'PST8PDT')
GROUP BY tz;
```

Outside the database, search application configuration for the same names: JDBC URLs and `-Duser.timezone`, `TZ` environment variables in containers and systemd units, ORM settings, and cron or scheduler definitions.

## Moving to City-Named Zones

For settings, switch to the city zone that matches what you meant:

```sql
ALTER DATABASE app SET timezone = 'America/New_York';
ALTER ROLE reporting SET timezone = 'America/Chicago';
-- postgresql.conf: timezone = 'America/Los_Angeles', then reload
```

New sessions pick up database and role settings at connect time; existing connections keep their value until they reconnect, so restart connection pools after the change.

For stored zone names, a direct mapping is safe for the US mainland zones:

```sql
UPDATE appointments
SET tz = CASE tz
           WHEN 'EST5EDT' THEN 'America/New_York'
           WHEN 'CST6CDT' THEN 'America/Chicago'
           WHEN 'MST7MDT' THEN 'America/Denver'
           WHEN 'PST8PDT' THEN 'America/Los_Angeles'
         END
WHERE tz IN ('EST5EDT', 'CST6CDT', 'MST7MDT', 'PST8PDT');
```

If you need a user's real location, the city zone may not be the right one either: someone in Indiana or Arizona has their own zone (`America/Indiana/Indianapolis`, `America/Phoenix`). The legacy names never captured that.

For stored instants (`timestamptz`) the question is which rules were used when they were written. Rows inserted with the old tzdata, where `EST5EDT` meant New York, are correct instants for New York local time; display them with `America/New_York` and they show what the user entered. Rows written by a session on the new tzdata with `EST5EDT` and a pre-1967 local time are one hour off compared with New York's actual clocks, if New York is what was meant. For those, reinterpret the instant:

```sql
-- instant written under new EST5EDT rules, meant as New York local time
SET timezone = 'UTC';
SELECT (ts AT TIME ZONE 'EST5EDT') AT TIME ZONE 'America/New_York' AS corrected
FROM (VALUES (timestamptz '1950-07-01 13:00+00')) v(ts);
-- 1950-07-01 12:00:00+00
```

## POSIX Strings and the Sign Trap

PostgreSQL also accepts full POSIX time zone specifications, and these are easy to confuse with the legacy names. On 18.7:

```sql
SET timezone = 'UTC';
SELECT ts AT TIME ZONE 'EST5EDT'                AS est5edt,
       ts AT TIME ZONE 'EST'                    AS est_fixed,
       ts AT TIME ZONE 'EST5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0' AS posix_full,
       ts AT TIME ZONE 'XYZ5ABC'                AS posix_no_rules
FROM (VALUES (timestamptz '1950-07-01 12:00+00'), (timestamptz '2027-07-15 12:00+00')) v(ts);
```

```
       est5edt       |      est_fixed      |     posix_full      |   posix_no_rules
---------------------+---------------------+---------------------+---------------------
 1950-07-01 07:00:00 | 1950-07-01 07:00:00 | 1950-07-01 08:00:00 | 1950-07-01 08:00:00
 2027-07-15 08:00:00 | 2027-07-15 07:00:00 | 2027-07-15 08:00:00 | 2027-07-15 08:00:00
```

- `EST` is a fixed UTC-5 with no daylight time, ever.
- `EST5EDT,M3.2.0,M11.1.0` applies today's rule (second Sunday of March to first Sunday of November) to every year, including 1950.
- A POSIX string without rules, such as `XYZ5ABC`, gets PostgreSQL's default US rules, again applied to every year.

None of them reproduce the history of `America/New_York`. And note the sign convention: POSIX offsets count hours west of UTC, so `SET timezone = 'UTC+5'` produces `-05`, the same as `Etc/GMT+5`:

```
    posix_sign_trap
------------------------
 2027-01-15 07:00:00-05
```

If you need a fixed offset, an ISO-style offset in the value (`'2027-01-15 12:00+05'`) or `interval` arithmetic is clearer than a POSIX zone name.

## Checking Other Systems

The same tzdata change reaches other software through its own update channel. Operating systems update `/usr/share/zoneinfo` through their `tzdata` package; Java has its own tzdata in the JDK (updated by JDK releases or the TZUpdater tool); Node.js and browsers use ICU's copy; Python's `zoneinfo` uses system files or the `tzdata` package. During the transition, two systems can disagree about 1950 in `EST5EDT` even when both are "correct" for their tzdata version. Moving to city-named zones everywhere removes that whole class of disagreement.

When comparing results from an updated and a not-yet-updated server, it helps to run the same query against both and look at the output side by side; [Chat2DB](https://chat2db.ai/download) lets you open both connections in one workspace and diff the result grids.

## Summary

Until tzdata 2026f, `EST5EDT`, `CST6CDT`, `MST7MDT` and `PST8PDT` behaved exactly like `America/New_York`, `America/Chicago`, `America/Denver` and `America/Los_Angeles`. Since PostgreSQL 18.7, 17.12, 16.16, 15.20 and 14.25, they follow US federal rules for all of history: no local mean time, and no local daylight time between 1920 and 1966. Current and future timestamps are unchanged, but historical timestamps can shift by an hour (or by a few minutes before 1883). Find the names in `pg_settings`, `pg_db_role_setting`, stored zone columns and application configuration, replace them with city-named zones, and store pure dates as `date` so that no time zone rule can move them.
